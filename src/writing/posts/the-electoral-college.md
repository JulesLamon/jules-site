The Electoral College is the United States' method of determining its President and Vice President. The Electoral College has faced much controversy and criticism since its inception in 1787 (Tanis) . The Electoral College is a complicated and multifaceted subject that must be approached with an understanding of its history, its impact on elections, and its relevance in the modern age. After examining all of these factors it is reasonable to state that the Electoral College is not the best election process available to the United States.

The Electoral College was established by the founding fathers at the Constitutional Convention in 1787. Placed in Article II, Section 1, of the Constitution the Electoral College has been the election system used by the United States since its creation, which isn’t necessarily a good thing. The Electoral College was a system created from compromise. The founding fathers knew from the beginning that the way the President and Vice President were elected was a crucial issue that they needed to decide on. However, they could not agree. On one side were those who believed that Congress should vote on the President and Vice President. However, the opposition argued that if Congress had this power it would lead to governmental corruption. The other idea proposed was that U.S. citizens would directly vote for the President and Vice President. However, many feared that too many citizens wouldn’t have the resources to make educated votes. After all, this was not the age of Google, information about Presidential candidates was not readily available. From this disagreement came the proposal of the Electoral College. Under the Electoral College, each state would be allocated a set number of electors, this number would be the combined total of its Senators and Representatives in Congress. This would give the people educated representatives who could cast well-educated votes in their stead. This would also protect smaller states who under a purely popular vote system would not have much influence over elections. (Roos)

The Electoral College has undergone many changes over the years. For example, in the 1800 election, Thomas Jefferson and Aaron Burr tied. Typically, the candidate with the greatest number of votes became President and the runner-up became Vice President. However, in this situation, there was no way of determining who should become President and who should become Vice President. This prompted the 12th amendment which had electors begin casting one vote for President and one vote for Vice President. (Tanis)

Criticisms of the Electoral College began in 1824 when Andrew Jackson lost the election, despite winning the popular vote. This led to a call for reform, but no changes were made. Ever since the Electoral College has been the subject of debate. Especially after the 1876, 1888, 2000, and 2016 elections in which the winner of the popular vote did not win the presidency. Currently 61% of the population support abolishing the Electoral College in favor of direct democracy (Brenan).

The Electoral College is supposed to be a system that represents the wants of the American people. However, this has not been the case in five different elections. In 1824, Andrew Jackson won by popular vote but lost because of the Electoral College. In 1876, Samuel Tilden won the popular vote but lost due to disputed electoral votes. In the 1888 election, Grover Cleveland won the popular vote but lost due to the Electoral College. In 2000, Al Gore won the popular vote but lost the presidency due to disputed electoral votes in Florida. Finally, in 2016 Hillary Clinton won the popular vote but lost due to the Electoral College (Roos). Another example in which U.S. citizens are not represented by the Electoral College is faithless electors. A faithless elector is an elector who pledges to vote for one candidate but then votes for another. This is a big example of misrepresentation of the public because electors are chosen based on who they say they will vote for.

When the Electoral College was first implemented the Founding Fathers opposed a system of direct voting because they worried voters wouldn’t be able to educate themselves about the candidates. However, in the modern age of the internet and social media, voters have more access to more information than ever before. It is with this realization that calls for the abolition of the Electoral College have grown louder. However, there are still some who argue that the Electoral College still serves a valuable purpose in the election process. The defenders of the Electoral College argue that the Electoral College ensures that smaller states have a voice in the election process. They also argue that the Electoral College forces candidates to campaign in a broad range of states instead of targeting the most populous ones.

However, one could argue that each citizen having a say in elections is more important than small states having more control than their population calls for. For example, if someone is a democrat living in a republican state their vote won’t have any influence over the election because their state’s votes are already devoted to the party the state’s majority is a part of. Proponents of the popular vote argue that it is a more democratic system and would better represent the U.S. population. Under the system of Popular vote, the President and Vice President would be decided by the majority of the U.S. citizens, instead of by the majority of the states.

Though the majority of the public is in favor of abolishing the Electoral College, this does not mean change will come easily. Disbanding the Electoral College and replacing it with a national popular vote faces significant challenges. Making any changes to the Electoral College requires adding an amendment to the Constitution, which is a complex and challenging process. Such a process seems even more daunting when one considers the current polarization that is occurring in the U.S. government. Adding an amendment would require a two-thirds majority vote of both the Senate and House of Representatives, as well as ratification by three-fourths of state legislatures. Because this would be such a difficult process many states have instead developed the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact (NPVIC). Several states have entered into this agreement, pledging to award their electoral votes to the national popular vote. With this initiative, states seek to replace the Electoral College with a popular vote system without having to go through the process of adding an amendment to the Constitution. However, this will only work if the states that have signed it represent a majority of the Electoral College, which is 270 votes. As of August 2023, the compact has been enacted in states that represent 205 electoral votes and is pending in states that represent 63 electoral votes (nationalpopularvote.com).

Ultimately the Electoral College has cost American citizens more than it has given them. When one considers the history of the Electoral College, its impact on elections, and its relevance in the modern age, one realizes that it is not the best alternative. While there are still many who oppose the use of the National Popular vote it would be a better representation of what the American people want than the Electoral College is. It can be conceded that the National Popular Vote system would have its drawbacks, but it fits modern politics better than the Electoral College. However, even with this being the case, making such a drastic change to the Constitution is a very difficult and time-consuming process. When one considers the current political climate it becomes even more inconceivable that such a change could be agreed upon. This is why the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact is such a promising option. If enough states sign it, the process of adding amendments to the Constitution could be circumvented while still allowing for a better system than the Electoral College. While it might be better to abolish the Electoral College completely, making changes to it is the more plausible alternative.
